# 11. Comparative Analysis: Choosing a Controller

The [§10](10_results.md) results established that all six controllers stabilize the single-magnet system: the five closed-form controllers (PID, SSC, Backstepping, FeedbackLin, SMC) settle at roughly sub-micrometer steady-state precision, and the offset-free NMPC reaches near-zero residual error under realistic conditions. This chapter addresses the more practical engineering question: **given a specific application context — number of degrees of freedom, model fidelity, real-time budget, tuning expertise, constraint requirements — which controller should you actually choose?** The comparison covers four dimensions — performance, implementation complexity, tuning complexity, and real-time cost — and closes with the scaling argument that motivates a single concrete recommendation for general-purpose magnetic bearing systems.

All six controllers from [§10](10_results.md) enter the comparison directly.

## 11.1 At-a-Glance Comparison

| | **PID** | **SSC** | **Backstepping** | **FeedbackLin** | **SMC** | **Offset-Free NMPC** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Transient performance** | Fast rise, very high overshoot | Slow rise, lowest overshoot among closed-form, smooth | Moderate rise, moderate overshoot | Moderate rise, moderate overshoot | Fast rise, best settling time in closed-form group | Slowest rise, ~zero overshoot |
| **Disturbance rejection** | Worst — slow recovery, large peak | Moderate — fast recovery, modest peak | Moderate | Moderate | Best in closed-form group | **Dominant** — peak 4–9× smaller, "never left band" |
| **Steady-state precision** | sub-µm (~0.6 µm) | sub-µm (~0.6 µm) | sub-µm (~0.6 µm) | sub-µm (~0.6 µm) | sub-µm (~0.6 µm) | **near-zero** (~0.1 µm, 6–7× better) |
| **Robustness to model error** | Low | Moderate | Low (exact cancellation) | Low (exact cancellation) | High | **Highest** (augmented disturbance state absorbs mismatch by construction) |
| **Implementation complexity** | Lowest | Low | Medium (recursive Lyapunov design) | Medium (Lie derivatives, sqrt, division) | Medium (sat function, sliding surface, integral state) | **Highest** (OCP formulation, augmented EKF, target calc, acados toolchain) |
| **Tuning knobs** | 3 design parameters ($\zeta, \omega_n, \alpha$) | $Q, R$ matrices (or pole locations) | 3 coefficients $(c_0, c_1, c_2)$ — coupled into gains | Same as SSC (outer loop only) | 5 parameters $(\lambda, k_i, \eta, K, \phi)$ — interacting | $Q, R, Q_N$, horizon $N$, sample time, plus augmented EKF noise tuning |
| **Real-time cost (per sample)** | Trivial (a handful of multiplications) | Trivial (matrix-vector product) | Moderate (similar to FeedbackLin) | Moderate (sqrt, division per step) | Moderate (sat, sqrt, conditional logic) | **Highest** (small NLP solved every sample via acados SQP-RTI) |
| **External dependency** | None | None | None | None | None | **acados** (required at build and runtime) |
| **MIMO / multi-DOF scaling** | Difficult (decentralized; ignores coupling) | **Excellent** (just a larger matrix) | Hard (recursion explodes with state coupling) | Hard (relative degree, decoupling matrix) | Hard (one surface per output, decoupling) | Hard (more disturbance states, larger OCP, solver cost grows with state dimension) |

## 11.2 Where Each Controller Excels

**PID** is the right choice when implementation simplicity dominates the specification, the operating range is small, and the team has stronger SISO than MIMO design experience. For a single-DOF demonstration with a forgiving plant, it remains an excellent baseline — the [§10](10_results.md) data even gives it the fastest rise time, although at the cost of by far the largest overshoot and an actuator peak roughly 3× any other controller.

**State-space control (SSC)** is the right choice when the operating range stays close to equilibrium, the model is reasonably well-known, and multiple states need to be coordinated through full feedback. Its tuning effort is comparable to PID (designer chooses pole locations or LQR weights), its real-time cost is essentially identical to PID (a matrix-vector product), and its scaling to higher dimensions is dramatically better than any of the nonlinear methods ([§11.3](11_comparative_analysis.md#113-the-multi-dof-question--the-structural-argument-for-ssc)).

**FeedbackLin and Backstepping** are the right choice when the operating range extends beyond the linearization neighborhood and the model parameters $K_m, m$ are known to good accuracy. The [§10](10_results.md) data showed these two perform essentially identically — confirming the structural-equivalence argument of [§4.3](04_backstepping.md#43-relationship-to-feedback-linearization) — so the choice between them comes down to which design philosophy the team is more comfortable with: geometric (Lie derivatives, exact cancellation) or recursive (Lyapunov design with virtual controls). Once one is implemented, the other contributes very little new performance.

**SMC** is the right choice when robustness against parametric uncertainty or external disturbances is the dominant requirement *and* an external solver dependency is unacceptable. The [§10](10_results.md) settling-time advantage and the intrinsic insensitivity to matched disturbances are real engineering payoffs — SMC is the best of the closed-form controllers at handling the disturbance event. The trade-off is tuning complexity (more parameters with stronger interactions) and the need for boundary-layer reasoning to avoid chattering. The non-obvious tuning constraint $\lambda > \sqrt{2g/x_0}$ documented in [§6.1](06_sliding_mode_control.md#61-sliding-surface) also makes the design less forgiving than the others.

**Offset-Free NMPC** is the right choice when any of these requirements is hard:
- *Constraints must be respected by construction* — input bounds, gap limits, or coupled state constraints. The closed-form controllers handle these externally via saturation or clamping, which means the controller is operating outside its design region whenever a constraint is hit. Offset-Free NMPC incorporates them directly into the OCP.
- *True offset-free behavior is required* — sub-µm residual error must approach zero, not merely become small. [§10.4](10_results.md#104-what-the-noise-on--noise-off-comparison-reveals) showed that integral action alone leaves a ~0.6 µm residual under a constant disturbance for this plant because of EKF linearization bias; only the augmented disturbance state $\hat{d}$ in Offset-Free NMPC drives this to ~0.1 µm.
- *Runtime operating-point flexibility is needed* — the reference $z_\text{ref}$ can be changed at runtime via the target-calculation block ([§7.5.3](07_nonlinear_mpc.md)) without redesigning the controller. The closed-form controllers would require re-running their design scripts at the new equilibrium.

The trade-offs are correspondingly steep: the **highest implementation complexity** of any controller in the comparison (OCP formulation + augmented EKF + target-calculation function + S-function compilation pipeline), the **highest real-time cost** (a small NLP solved every sample at the chosen rate), and the **only controller with an external solver dependency** (acados at both build and runtime). These costs are documented as engineering reality, not as a knock against the method — they are simply what offset-free MPC requires.

## 11.3 The Multi-DOF Question — The Structural Argument for SSC

The single-magnet system is 1-DOF, which obscures a major practical consideration: **how do these controllers scale when the system has multiple degrees of freedom?** Active magnetic bearings for rotating machinery typically have 4–5 DOF; general-purpose maglev systems are often 6-DOF. The six controllers scale very differently:

- **PID** scales by replication — one decentralized loop per axis. This ignores cross-axis coupling and becomes inadequate as the dynamics couple more strongly. A truly MIMO PID exists in principle but loses the implementational simplicity that justifies PID in the first place.
- **SSC scales linearly**. A 6-DOF problem produces a state vector of position + velocity + integral states (typically 18 entries); the design workflow is *structurally identical* to the 1-D case, just with larger matrices. MATLAB's `place`, `lqr`, and `kalman` all work natively in MIMO; pole placement and LQR are no more conceptually difficult in 18 dimensions than in 3. The dual-magnet design ([§12](12_dual_magnet_levitation.md)) and the sled-unit design ([§13](13_sled_unit_levitation.md)) are concrete demonstrations: extending SSC from 1-D to higher dimensions required only redefinitions of the augmented system matrix, with no structural change to the implementation.
- **Backstepping in MIMO** loses the clean recursive structure as soon as states become coupled. Each virtual control depends on the partial derivatives of all preceding virtual controls, and the resulting symbolic expressions grow combinatorially.
- **FeedbackLin in MIMO** requires choosing outputs with matched relative degree, computing the decoupling matrix, and dealing with singularities of the input transformation. The complexity grows superlinearly with DOF, and the design becomes increasingly bespoke.
- **SMC in MIMO** requires designing one sliding surface per controlled output, with explicit decoupling design between them. Workable, but rarely elegant; chattering also becomes harder to manage across multiple surfaces simultaneously.
- **Offset-Free NMPC in MIMO** scales the worst of the methods compared. The OCP grows with the augmented state dimension (more decision variables, more constraints); the augmented EKF carries one disturbance state per controlled output to preserve the offset-free observability requirement; and the solver cost per sample increases nonlinearly with state and input dimensions. At high sample rates and multi-DOF count, real-time feasibility becomes a serious constraint that must be checked empirically rather than assumed.

This is the structural argument for state-space control as a default choice in magnetic bearing applications: **its complexity is essentially DOF-independent**, while the nonlinear analytical methods scale poorly enough that they become bespoke design exercises at the DOF count of practical interest, and the optimization-based Offset-Free NMPC scales worst because it pays the multi-DOF cost in both design effort *and* runtime solver cost.

## 11.4 Recommendation

For a single-DOF demonstration or research-scale plant, any of the six controllers is appropriate, and the choice is driven by what the project aims to demonstrate. The [§10](10_results.md) results favor Offset-Free NMPC on raw performance metrics — best disturbance rejection by a wide margin, near-zero steady-state error, lowest overshoot — but the implementation and runtime trade-offs documented above mean Offset-Free NMPC is not automatically the "right" answer in every context.

**For a general-purpose magnetic levitation or active magnetic bearing system with multiple degrees of freedom, state-space control with integral action is the most practical default**:

- It scales naturally and cleanly to MIMO without structural redesign.
- Its tuning effort grows minimally — a designer who can apply `lqr` to a 1-D system can apply it to a 6-D system.
- Its real-time cost stays a matrix-vector product, with predictable timing across embedded hardware.
- It pairs cleanly with the same EKF observer ([§8](08_ekf.md)), re-derived once for the higher-dimensional plant, keeping the full sensing-and-control stack consistent.

The nonlinear closed-form methods (FeedbackLin, Backstepping, SMC) remain compelling for specialized applications — large operating ranges, dominant model uncertainty, hard real-time disturbance rejection — and should be added *on top of* an SSC baseline when those specific needs arise, rather than chosen *instead of* SSC.

**Offset-Free NMPC, now documented as a working controller in [§7.5](07_nonlinear_mpc.md), becomes attractive whenever any of these is a hard requirement: explicit constraint handling, true offset-free behavior (residual error approaching zero rather than merely becoming small), or runtime operating-point flexibility.** The price is the implementation complexity, the runtime solver cost, and the external acados dependency documented in [§11.1](11_comparative_analysis.md#111-at-a-glance-comparison), together with the multi-DOF scaling caveats of [§11.3](11_comparative_analysis.md#113-the-multi-dof-question--the-structural-argument-for-ssc). For applications where the DOF count is small (1–2), the sample rate is moderate, and one of the offset-free requirements is non-negotiable, Offset-Free NMPC is the right tool. For higher-DOF systems at high sample rates where sub-µm residual error is acceptable, SSC + an EKF remains the more practical choice.

The dual-magnet model in [§12](12_dual_magnet_levitation.md) and the sled-unit model in [§13](13_sled_unit_levitation.md) demonstrate this recommendation concretely: extending SSC from one magnet to two, and then to five DOFs with eight magnets, was at each stage a straightforward extension of the augmented-state matrix; redoing the equivalent extensions for FeedbackLin, Backstepping, or Offset-Free NMPC would have required reconstructing each design from scratch.
